UTC vs GMT: What's the Difference?

For everyday purposes, GMT and UTC are the same time — both sit at offset UTC+0. The difference is what each one is:

  • GMT (Greenwich Mean Time) is a time zone — historically the mean solar time at the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, London. It is still the legal winter time of the UK and is used as a zone name by several countries.
  • UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) is a time standard, not a zone. It is kept by atomic clocks worldwide and adjusted with leap seconds so it never drifts more than a second from Earth's rotation. Every time zone offset on Earth — UTC−5, UTC+9 — is measured from UTC.

Which should you use?

In software, aviation, science and anything international, use UTC — offsets like "UTC−5" are unambiguous all year. GMT survives in everyday UK usage and in the phrase "GMT" on older clocks and watches. One trap: London is only on GMT in winter; in summer it is on BST (UTC+1), while UTC itself never changes. Reykjavik, which keeps UTC+0 all year, genuinely matches GMT in every month.

Every offset on this site is expressed against UTC, computed from the IANA database.

Frequently asked questions

Is GMT the same as UTC?

In clock terms, yes — both read the same time. GMT is a time zone; UTC is the atomic standard that all offsets are measured from.

Is London on GMT or UTC?

London uses GMT in winter and British Summer Time (UTC+1) in summer. UTC is not a place's time at all — it is the reference standard.

Why do offsets say UTC−5 instead of GMT−5?

Because UTC is the fixed reference. GMT is one zone among many (and zones move relative to it when they observe daylight saving); UTC never moves.
