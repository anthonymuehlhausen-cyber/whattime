Meeting Across Time Zones Without the 3 AM Mistake

Every cross-zone scheduling failure comes from the same three traps: a fixed offset remembered from the wrong season, a bare "3 PM" with no zone, and a recurring meeting that silently shifts when one side changes clocks. Here is how to avoid all three.

1. Find the overlap, not just the difference

Work out both cities' office hours in one reference time. New York 9–5 is London 2–10 PM almost all year — the gap is 5 hours in winter (EST vs GMT) and still 5 hours in summer (EDT vs BST), because both sides spring forward. The comfortable shared window is roughly 9 AM–1 PM New York / 2–6 PM London. Only during the few in-between weeks each March and October, when one side has changed clocks and the other has not, does the gap narrow to 4 hours and 9 AM New York become 1 PM London. New York to Sydney has almost no office-hours overlap at all, which is why those meetings rotate the pain or go async. Our converter shows the day shift explicitly, which is where most mistakes hide.

2. Write times so they cannot be misread

Always attach a city, not just an abbreviation: "3 PM New York" survives daylight saving; "3 PM EST" in July is an hour of ambiguity. For international groups, add the UTC time in parentheses — UTC never changes, so it anchors everyone.

3. Re-check around the clock changes

The dangerous weeks are mid-March to early April and late October to early November, when North America and Europe are briefly on different offsets than usual (2026 dates). Recurring meetings pinned to one side's clock drift an hour for everyone else. A 30-second converter check on those weeks prevents the classic empty-call-at-8-AM.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best time for a New York–London meeting?

Around 9 AM–1 PM New York time (2–6 PM London) — the overlap of both workdays in most months.

Why did my recurring meeting move by an hour?

One side changed clocks and the other did not (yet). This happens every March and October/November for a few weeks between the US and Europe.

Should I write EST or ET in meeting invites?

Write ET (or the city name). EST specifically means UTC−5, which is wrong for the East Coast from March to November.
